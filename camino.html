01 — Max two weeks

Five ways to use 22 Feb – 7 Mar.

every option fits in 14 days

If the trip is capped at two weeks, the full Francés below doesn't fit — so each option here is a 2-week cut that starts on 22 February and is home by 7 March. The table compares them on what matters in winter; the cards give the feel of each. Our pick is A, with its day-by-day under the table.

A · our pick · earns the Compostela

Sarria → Santiago → Finisterre

Last 100 km of the Francés + the Atlantic epilogue

≈179 km · 8 walking days · walk 23 Feb – 3 Mar · home by 7 Mar · ≈ €0.8–1.2k

The classic two-week Camino: fly to Santiago, bus to Sarria, walk the last 100 km (that's what makes the Compostela official), then three more days to the lighthouse at Finisterre. Galicia in February is cool and rainy but rarely dangerous, and this is the best-supported stretch in winter.

B · milder, coastal

Porto → Santiago, coastal

Camino Portugués, coastal variant

≈274 km · 11–12 days · walk 23 Feb – 5/6 Mar · ≈ €1.0–1.5k

Atlantic beaches and boardwalks from Porto, then inland through Galicia to Santiago. The mildest weather of the walking options; arrives right at the two-week edge, so book the Porto flight early.

C · the big crossing

Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port → Burgos

First half of the Francés: Pyrenees, Navarra, Rioja

≈294 km · 12–13 days · walk 22 Feb – 5 Mar · ≈ €1.0–1.4k

The Pyrenees crossing (Valcarlos route in winter — the high route is closed), the Alto del Perdón, Logroño's tapas street, and a big arrival under Burgos' cathedral. No Compostela (you stop short of Santiago), but the highest scenery-per-week of the five.

D · quiet northern Galicia

Vilalba → Santiago

Last 100 km of the Camino del Norte

≈102 km · 7 days · walk 23 Feb – 1 Mar · ≈ €0.7–1.1k

Rías, Mondoñedo, Guitiriz — green Galicia approached from the north coast; wetter and quieter than the Francés's last 100 km, same Compostela. Cheap and compact; pair with a day in Lugo.

E · the warm escape

Madeira levada week

Funchal → levadas & ridges (Pico do Arieiro, Pico Ruivo) → Funchal

≈70 km of walks · 7 days · 22 Feb – 1 Mar · ≈ €0.9–1.3k

15–20 °C in February, subtropical green, waterfall and ridge walks — no pilgrimage, no Compostela, and short, but the best weather of the five by far. Pick this if February cold is the dealbreaker.

How to choose within two weeks

With 14 days, aim for one walking goal plus buffers for flights and a rest day — not five goals. A and D both earn the Compostela; A adds the ocean finish and has the deepest winter bed network.

  • Want the Compostela + the Finisterre sunset on a first Camino → A: Sarria → Santiago → Finisterre (day-by-day below).
  • Want the most kilometres in two weeks → B (Porto coastal) or C (SJPP → Burgos).
  • Want the quietest / cheapest Camino → D (Vilalba → Santiago); the Vía de la Plata's last 100 km from Ourense is the same idea inland.
  • February cold is the dealbreaker → E: Madeira.

02 — The full route · ~5 weeks

Why this route, why now.

Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port → Santiago → Finisterre

The Francés is the classic pilgrimage road: ~780 km from the French Pyrenees to Galicia, crossing five landscapes — mountains, green valleys, wine country, the high meseta, and the Atlantic forests. We start on 22 February on purpose: the trails are empty, the air is cold and clear, and the walk feels like it belongs to you alone. This is the full ~5-week version — with a two-week cap, use option A in the comparison above (Sarria → Santiago → Finisterre).

Walk far, not fast

20–29 km a day at an easy pace. Thirty-four walking days with two full rest days in Burgos and León. The Camino rewards the unhurried — you are not racing the route, you are reading it.

Climb where it counts

The scenic high points are all here: the Pyrenees crossing on day one (Valcarlos route in winter), the Alto del Perdón wind ridge, the Cruz de Ferro at 1,505 m, and a night in O Cebreiro at 1,293 m above Galicia.

Arrive slow

Santiago deserves a day of its own — the cathedral, the pilgrim’s mass, the Compostela. Then the epilogue: three more days and 63 km west to Finisterre, where the road ends at the ocean.

Why 22 February is the right time to start

Winter changes the terms of the walk — and for the right pilgrim, that is the invitation, not the warning. You trade crowds for silence, and summer’s race for beds for a quieter, rawer pilgrimage.

  • Empty trails — in February the Francés can feel like yours alone.
  • The high Pyrenees route is closed (1 Nov – 31 Mar), so day one takes the beautiful Valcarlos valley route instead — gentler and safer.
  • Beds are scarcer: most albergues close in winter, so we book ahead and lean on private albergues and pensions.
  • Short days (~11 h of light): start at 7:30–8:00, plan water and food gaps, carry microspikes.

03 — The route

Eight hundred km, drawn.

schematic map — not to scale

From the Basque foothills over the Pyrenees into Navarra, east to Pamplona, then a long westward line through La Rioja, across the meseta of Castile to León, over the Bierzo mountains into Galicia, down the last 100 km to the cathedral — then on to the sea.

Camino Francés — main line (34 walking days) overnight stops epilogue to Finisterre (+3 days)

Two big climbs define the walk: the Pyrenees crossing on day one (the winter Valcarlos route tops out near Roncesvalles at ~950 m — lower than the closed high route, but still mountain weather), and the long pull into Galicia ending at O Cebreiro, 1,293 m, on day 29. Everything between is rolling plateau and valley — the meseta is flat, windy, and beautiful in its emptiness.

05 — Practical

Everything you need before you go.

winter edition · Feb–Mar

01Weather & daylight

22 Feb – 1 Apr · what winter actually feels like

  • Pyrenees (day 1): 0–8 °C, possible snow or ice — microspikes essential.
  • Meseta (days 14–22): −2–12 °C, hard wind, big sun. Layers and a windproof shell.
  • Galicia (days 30+): 5–14 °C and rain is likely — waterproof boots and a pack cover.
  • Daylight: ~11 h on 22 Feb growing to ~12.5 h by late March. Sunrise ≈ 7:50, sunset ≈ 18:50 (CET). Start walking 7:30–8:00.

02The Pyrenees in winter

the one rule you must not break

  • The Route Napoléon is closed 1 Nov – 31 Mar (fines up to €12,000). The official winter line is the Valcarlos valley route — ~25 km Saint-Jean → Roncesvalles, open year-round.
  • Split it at Valcarlos (~13 km in) if the cold or dark argues for it; there is a winter albergue there.
  • Check the status board at the Pilgrim Office in Saint-Jean (39 rue de la Citadelle) the morning you leave. Never attempt the high route during closure.

03Beds in winter

book ahead — this is the one thing winter demands

  • Many albergues close November–March. Use the APRINCA winter list plus Wise Pilgrim / Buen Camino / Gronze apps; phone the night before to confirm.
  • Private albergues and pensions stay open and take bookings (phone, WhatsApp, website). Public albergues are walk-up only, one night, credencial required.
  • Prices: Xunta (Galicia) €10 · municipal €8–12 · private €15–27 · pilgrim dinner €10–16.

05Packing — 6–7 kg

carry light, walk far · leave room for the stone at Cruz de Ferro

  • 35–40 L pack + rain cover
  • Waterproof boots (Feb mud/snow) or grippy trail runners
  • Microspikes (Pyrenees)
  • Rain shell + insulated jacket
  • 2× merino base layers, 2× socks
  • Headlamp (early starts)
  • 2 L water capacity
  • Blister kit (Compeed), plasters, painkillers
  • Credencial del peregrino + passport
  • Phone, power bank, offline maps
  • Quick-dry towel, soap, sunscreen
  • Earplugs (dorms), light sandals

06Getting there & away

logistics

  • In: fly to Biarritz (BIQ) — bus/taxi ~1 h to Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port — or Pamplona (PNA) / Lourdes, then bus to SJPP.
  • Out: Santiago de Compostela airport (SCQ) has direct flights, or ALSA bus / Renfe train to Madrid.
  • Epilogue: Tralusa bus Santiago → Negreira / Finisterre (~1 h); return to Santiago the same or next day.
  • Escape hatches: ALSA buses and Renfe trains run along the whole route — a sore knee is not the end of the pilgrimage.

07Paperwork & the Compostela

the bureaucracy, simplified

  • Pick up the credencial (free) at the SJPP Pilgrim Office before you start; stamp it twice a day (café + albergue).
  • The Compostela requires the last 100 km on foot — Sarria onward qualifies, and we walk ~130 km from there.
  • Collect it at the Pilgrim’s Reception Office, rúa das Carretas 33, Santiago (10:00–18:00 daily).
  • Pilgrim’s Mass in the cathedral at 12:00 daily; the Botafumeiro swings on Fridays and holy days.

08Health & safety

boring but read it once

  • 112 is the EU emergency number everywhere on the route; farmacias show a green cross.
  • Travel insurance with trekking/hiking cover — the Pyrenees and O Cebreiro days are the exposed ones.
  • Day 18 (Carrión → Terradillos) has 17 km with no water or shops — carry 2 L and food. Same care on El Burgo → Reliegos.
  • Download offline maps; signal fades on the meseta and in Galician valleys.

06 — The epilogue

Then walk to the end of the world.

optional but recommended

Santiago → Negreira → Olveiroa → Finisterre

For nine centuries pilgrims walked past Santiago to the Atlantic and burned their clothes at Cape Finisterre — the Romans called it the end of the world. Three more days (63 km) take you there: forest paths, the last corredoiras, and on the final afternoon the lighthouse above the waves. Watch the sunset, then catch the bus back to Santiago.
